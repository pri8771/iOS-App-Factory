## Original Prompt

```
Build a native iOS Solitaire (Klondike) game — SwiftUI, 100% local, zero
network calls, zero sign-in/accounts/analytics/telemetry of any kind. No
backend. All state (stats, settings, saved game) persists on-device only.

THE CENTRAL CONSTRAINT — READ THIS FIRST: this must be a real, fully
playable, correctly-rules-implemented Klondike solitaire game. Drag-and-drop
(or tap-to-move) card interaction, legal-move validation, win detection,
undo, new-game/deal, and a stock/waste/foundation/tableau layout that
actually works. Do not let the visual ambition come at the cost of the game
being playable and correct — a beautiful app that can't be played is a
failure.

VISUAL DESIGN — THIS IS THE PART THAT MUST WIN AWARDS:
Cards must look like real, tactile playing cards — not the generic flat
rounded-rectangles-with-tiny-corner-pips that every solitaire clone uses.
Invent a genuinely original custom card face system: bespoke rank/suit
typography or iconography (not SF Symbols reused as suit glyphs), a
consistent illustrated or generative pattern language across all 52 cards
and the card back, real card-stock texture/shadow/lighting, and satisfying
physical motion (cards should feel like they have weight — spring-based
drag, believable flip/deal animation, subtle parallax or depth).

The bar: this should look and feel like something from an Apple Design
Award reel, not a tutorial app. Judge yourself against that bar throughout
— if a screen looks like a stock SwiftUI solitaire tutorial, it has failed
the brief. One clear, original visual "hook" (an art direction, a material
language, a signature interaction) should be identifiable in one glance
that no other solitaire app has.

Also include: light/dark mode (both fully art-directed, not just inverted
colors), haptics on card moves/wins, a stats screen (games played/won,
best time, streak — all local), and a settings screen (draw-1 vs draw-3,
card back selection, timer on/off).

Ship something that could realistically be submitted to the App Store today
and be visually distinctive in a screenshot alone.
```

### Round 1


**Codex (Product Strategist · User Advocate) — Round 1**

A user-facing promise of this app is to deliver a single-device, always-offline SwiftUI Klondike solitaire game that is truly playable with correct rules, undo and win detection, plus high-design card visuals and tactile motion, while keeping all data local to the device.

The primary user is a casual mobile player who wants a quick, reliable five-minute session and an occasional design-minded player who judges first-screen quality before deciding whether to continue, in moments like commuting or waiting where interruptions and app switching are common, so the app must resume quickly into the active game instead of forcing navigation setup.

The core loop is: pick/confirm draw mode in settings, start or resume a real game, drag/tap legal cards through tableau/foundations/stock-waste, get immediate move feedback and haptics, undo when needed, and either complete a valid Klondike win condition or restart with a fresh properly shuffled game while stats/streak/time update locally.

Hard scope boundaries are: one app only (`solitaire-atelier`) with Game/Stats/Settings tabs, no multiplayer/social/leaderboards, no other solitaire variants, no iCloud or cross-device sync, no accounts, telemetry, ads, IAP, Game Center, widgets, or live activities, no third-party framework dependency for core card logic, and draw-mode change applies only via new game while card-back changes can be live cosmetic changes.

Measurable success criteria are: legal move validation and state transitions are deterministic and verifiable against standard single-deck Klondike rules (7 tableau, 4 foundations, king-to-empty), draw-1 and draw-3 behavior is locked by explicit in-app rules and cannot corrupt in-progress games when toggled, timer-on sessions start on first action and pause in background, win is detected only when all foundations are complete, undo restores exact prior full game state, game/settings/stats persist across relaunch without mid-animation state corruption, stats are per draw mode with “N/A” best-time whenever timing is disabled, and the card face system is readable at native card size (~45–50pt width) with explicit hit-tested drag targets and VoiceOver labels.

The decision that matters most in this phase is to prioritize playability-correctness determinism first and let visual flourishes follow it; the biggest objection is that over-emphasizing the first-build “wow” motion can quietly ship interaction debt in drag/drop and touch ergonomics, so the phase output must preserve the rule that “award-like visuals are only valid if the game is genuinely playable and recoverable under real interruption, drag, and legal/illegal move conditions.”

**Claude (Design Lead · Systems Thinker) — Round 1**

Here's my read on this, coming at it as the person who has to actually draw the screens and think through what happens when things go sideways.

The product promise, stated as tight as I can make it: a single native iOS Klondike solitaire game that plays a completely correct hand of cards with a bespoke, tactile card-face system you won't mistake for any other solitaire app, and that never asks you to sign in, go online, or trust anything it can't prove happened on your own device.

Primary user and scenario: someone picks up their phone for a five-to-fifteen-minute session — waiting room, commute, before bed — opens the app, and it drops them straight back into whatever game they were in the middle of, or straight into a fresh deal if they finished or abandoned the last one. There is no menu screen standing between "tap the icon" and "see cards." That's the single most important IA decision this phase needs to lock, because it's the thing that separates "reliable pocket game" from "app I have to relearn navigation for every time."

Core loop, concretely: deal → look at the board → move a card (tap or drag) → get instant legal/illegal feedback (haptic + visual) → repeatedly draw from stock and place cards until either you win (foundations complete, animated payoff, stats update) or you decide to bail via New Game → the game either auto-persists silently in the background or the player explicitly starts over. Undo sits inside this loop as a safety net, not a separate mode — it has to feel like "take that back" during the same session, not a history browser.

The three screens (Game, Stats, Settings) are peers per the earlier tab decision, but I want to flag the game-over moment as its own state that needs explicit design attention now, because it's easy to hand-wave: what does the screen literally show between "last card lands on foundation" and "back to a playable board"? There needs to be a win state (full-screen or overlay celebration, stats already updated behind it, path back to a new game) and there needs to be an explicit "no more moves" affordance — Klondike frequently ends not in a win but in a stuck board, and if the app never tells the player that, they'll sit there confused whether they're bad at the game or the game is broken. That's a state this phase should name as required even though the original prompt never mentions it.

The scope boundary I want written down plainly: this is one app, one variant, one deck, portrait-only, entirely on-device, and the win condition is the only thing that can be claimed as an outcome — no fake "streak keeps going" or best-time claims not actually backed by a completed, timed game. Everything about card art, motion, and material language is real but secondary to the rules engine; if a design idea and a rules requirement ever collide, the rules requirement wins and the collision gets written down rather than quietly resolved in the art's favor.

For success criteria I'd rather name things a build can actually be checked against than vibes: every legal move in both draw modes is accepted and every illegal move is rejected with feedback every time; a full game can be played start-to-win with foundations correctly completed; undo restores exact prior state across at least a full sequence of moves, not just one step back; force-quitting mid-drag and relaunching shows the same logical board a player last saw settled, never a corrupted or duplicated pile; stats and settings survive relaunch; and a card can be identified by rank and suit at actual on-screen size without squinting — that legibility test isn't optional polish, it's a pass/fail gate on whether the custom card system is even usable.

The risk I'd put my name next to: the "resume-active-game as default" decision is elegant but it means there is effectively no "start" screen to hide first-run rough edges behind — the very first launch experience *is* the polished bespoke card table, immediately, with no loading screen to buy time. If the deal animation, card rendering, or layout has any hitch, it's visible on the first three seconds of first use, every time. That raises the stakes on the deal/flip animation being the signature hook — it's not just the nicest moment, it's also the least forgiving one, because it's mandatory and unskippable on every cold launch.
